  1. VA-ECMO · Differential hypoxemiaScoping reviewRev Cardiovasc MedFirst published September 30 · Revisited
    Priority: HighClinical usefulness: 4.4/5

    VA-ECMO watershed physiology: cannulation configuration matters more than simply increasing flow

    Study: The Aortic Watershed Phenomenon and Differential Hypoxemia During Peripheral Venoarterial ECMO: A Scoping Review of Clinical and Experimental Evidence.

    Design: PRISMA-ScR scoping review of clinical and experimental literature searched in MEDLINE and EMBASE. Of 245 records identified, 16 studies were included. First published online September 30, 2026 (DOI: 10.31083/RCM52934).

    Key findings

    • Reported differential hypoxemia frequency ranged from 1.5% to 13.3% across included studies. This is a heterogeneous range, not a reliable pooled incidence estimate.
    • When native cardiac output recovers while pulmonary oxygenation remains poor, the aortic mixing interface may shift distally. Hypoxemic native blood can then supply the coronary and supra-aortic arteries even if femoral arterial oxygenation looks adequate.
    • Configuration-targeted approaches reported improvements in upper-body oxygenation or perfusion surrogates: move venous drainage toward the SVC/upper right atrium, convert to VAV-ECMO with controlled flow splitting and internal-jugular oxygenated return, or move arterial return toward a more proximal/antegrade site.
    • Beyond comparing upper- and lower-body oximetry and blood gases, the included literature described contrast-enhanced ultrasound, Doppler assessment, and positional CT angiography to examine mixing. Experimental models also showed why higher ECMO flow alone may not resolve the problem as native output increases.
    Perfusionist briefing: In femoral VA-ECMO, assess right-hand saturation/right-radial arterial blood gases together with lower-body and circuit oxygenation, native ejection, and pulmonary status. A normal post-oxygenator or femoral gas does not exclude cerebral or myocardial hypoxemia. If differential hypoxemia develops, identify the underlying mixing and drainage problem before treating RPM escalation as the only solution. SVC-oriented drainage, VAV conversion, and antegrade return are configuration options requiring individualized cannula and flow assessment—not interchangeable bedside fixes.

    Limitations: The 16 included studies used different definitions, detection thresholds, designs, and populations. Evidence for configuration changes is mainly physiological or based on small clinical series; the review does not prove that any one strategy improves survival or neurologic outcomes. Right-radial sampling is an important upper-body monitor but cannot directly establish coronary oxygenation.

  2. CPB circuit · Surface coatingIn-vitro comparative studyAsian Cardiovasc Thorac AnnFirst published September 2 · Revisited
    Priority: ModerateClinical usefulness: 2.9/5

    Polymer-coated circuits showed lower lactate in vitro—but this is not evidence of better clinical perfusion

    Study: Hemocompatible polymer coating for extracorporeal circulation circuit affects lactate levels during cardiopulmonary bypass surgery.

    Design: Small human whole-blood in-vitro comparison of polymer-coated (PC), heparin-coated (HC), and noncoated (NC) membrane-oxygenator circuits (reported n=5). Heparin diluted in a glucose solution was continuously administered; lactate, glucose, oxygen partial pressure, and oxygen saturation were measured at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 hours. First published online September 2, 2026 (DOI: 10.1177/02184923261485742).

    Key findings

    • Mean lactate was significantly lower in PC circuits than in HC and NC circuits at the sampled time points; HC and NC did not differ significantly in lactate.
    • Mean glucose was lower in NC circuits than in PC or HC circuits; PC and HC did not differ significantly in glucose.
    • Oxygen partial pressure and oxygen saturation did not differ significantly among the coating groups.
    • The abstract does not provide absolute lactate differences or patient outcomes, so the size and bedside relevance of the effect cannot be established from these results.
    Perfusionist briefing: Circuit surface treatment may affect measured metabolic markers under controlled circulation conditions, independent of the oxygenation measures reported here. During clinical CPB, however, a rising lactate must still be evaluated with indexed oxygen delivery, pump flow, hemoglobin, perfusion pressure, temperature, glucose administration, and the patient's condition. These bench data do not justify choosing a coating based on lactate alone or interpreting lactate reduction as proven tissue-perfusion benefit.

    Limitations: This was a small bench experiment, not a randomized clinical CPB study. Prolonged experimental circulation, glucose-containing heparin infusion, and the absence of patient-level endpoints limit clinical generalizability. The mechanisms of lower lactate and glucose remain uncertain; hemocompatibility as the cause was not directly established.
