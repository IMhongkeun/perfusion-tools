Quick reference notes

Quick reference guidance

  • These cards provide rapid lookup values for intraoperative use.
  • Ranges vary by protocol, patient physiology, and monitoring targets.
  • Always integrate pressure, NIRS/EEG, and lab trends when titrating flow.
  • This tool is not a medical device and does not replace clinical judgment.
  • Content is data-driven for easy updates; the evidence review date and the source of each value are listed under Sources & evidence levels.
  • Educational use only; follow institutional and surgeon-directed protocols.

ACP right radial pressure rationale

  • Right radial artery pressure is commonly used as a surrogate for cerebral perfusion pressure during unilateral ACP (e.g., Kazui technique; right axillary cannulation in the 2024 EACTS/EACTAIC/EBCP guideline, section 5.1).
  • Unilateral ACP (often via innominate or right axillary/subclavian) directly supplies the right carotid/right brain.
  • Reported targets: adjust flow to keep right radial pressure ~40–60 mmHg (or 40–70); see Sources & evidence levels.
  • Left radial artery pressure can be lower due to collateral flow and may under-represent left-sided perfusion during unilateral ACP.

ACP detail (adult reference)

  • Flow rate: 8–12 mL/kg/min (≈0.6 mL/min/g cerebral tissue in some references).
  • Perfusion pressure: 40–60 mmHg (right radial artery reference).
  • Temperature: 23–28°C moderate hypothermia.
  • pH management: Alpha-stat to support cerebral autoregulation.
  • Duration: no universal safe limit is established. Circulatory arrest up to ~80 min with ACP was reported without a significant increase in mortality or stroke in an elective observational registry—if >40–50 min is expected, consider bilateral ACP.
  • Monitoring: NIRS/EEG with baseline/trend and bilateral symmetry emphasis.

ACP detail (pediatric reference)

  • Flow rate: 40–80 mL/kg/min (reference ~50–64). Observed means: 46 ± 6 mL/kg/min at ~25°C in children <5 y (72% neonates); 56.6 ± 10.6 mL/kg/min at 18°C in neonates.
  • Perfusion pressure: titrate (often 20–25 mmHg reported; higher MAP targets used in some centers).
  • Perfusate temp: 18–25°C. ACP has been reported at 18°C and at ~25°C; deep hypothermia with ACP has been typical in North American practice.
  • pH management: pH-stat should be considered for neonates and infants undergoing hypothermia during CPB (AmSECT pediatric Guideline 9.1).
  • Duration: no universal safe limit is established. Observed ACP times: median 48 min (range 10–123) at ~25°C; mean 71 ± 28 min (range 5–121) at 18°C—plan the ACP strategy and avoid DHCA when possible.
  • Monitoring: Bilateral NIRS (trend-focused) ± EEG; TCD optional (availability dependent).
  • Hct: neonatal/infant arch ACP + hypothermia commonly uses ~30–35% as a reference range.
  • Higher Hct raises O₂ content but increases viscosity—adjust with pressure/flow plus NIRS/EEG response and follow institutional protocols.

RCP detail (reference)

  • SVC pressure: maintenance reference ~20–25 mmHg; adjust to the measurement site and neuromonitoring. Excessive pressure risks brain edema.
  • Flow: pressure-driven; 300–500 mL/min commonly cited to maintain SVC pressure ~20–25 mmHg.
  • Monitoring: NIRS/EEG with baseline/trend focus and bilateral symmetry.
  • TCD can be added to track cerebral blood flow velocity.

HCA safety-time notes

  • Varies by center, patient factors (age, comorbidities), neuromonitoring (NIRS baseline/trend with EEG), and use of ACP/RCP.
  • Always minimize circulatory arrest time.
  • Cooling time: at least 20–50 min (practice range; no guideline minimum identified).
  • Rewarming: ≤0.5°C/min is reasonable when arterial outlet temperature is ≥30°C (STS/SCA/AmSECT 2015, Class IIa, Level B). The 2024 EACTS/EACTAIC/EBCP guideline gives no numeric rewarming rate.
  • Table times are historical cerebral-metabolism model estimates for arrest without cerebral perfusion (McCullough 1999), not clinically validated safe limits. Values marked * are calculated with the same model assumptions (5 min tolerated at 37°C; Q10 2.3): time = 5 × 2.3(37 − T)/10 min. Temperature categories follow the 2024 EACTS/EACTAIC/EBCP guideline (core rectal/bladder temperature). See Sources & evidence levels below.
  • Hypothermia-only arrest estimates do not directly apply when ACP is used. Assess cerebral protection using perfusion adequacy and neuromonitoring. If lower-body perfusion is interrupted, consider lower-body ischemic time separately. Temperature alone does not define a universal safe duration.
  • Follow institutional protocols and patient monitoring; educational use only.

MUF detail (clinical reference)

  • Role / Indication: Pediatric & congenital: Consider when no contraindications exist (AmSECT Guideline 16.1). Adult: Recommended in high-risk or anaemic patients to reduce bleeding and transfusion (2024 EACTS/EACTAIC/EBCP, Class I, Level A).
  • Blood flow through the filter: Pediatric: 10–20 mL/kg/min. Adult: 150–300 mL/min in a study protocol. Filtrate removal rate is a separate setting. Neonates/low-weight infants: Avoid abrupt flow changes; titrate carefully to monitoring trends.
  • Monitoring / Titration (practical): Titrate MUF to NIRS + CVP + arterial pressure trends and overall hemodynamics.
  • Core safety rule (critical): Maintain positive arterial line pressure at all times to reduce air entrainment/embolism risk. Practical controls may include shunt/clamp management and centrifugal pump RPM adjustments to preserve a favorable pressure gradient.
  • Duration / Endpoint: Typical duration: 10–20 min (adult commonly ~10–15 min). Continue until target Hct and/or desired net fluid removal is achieved; adjust to hemodynamics and monitoring trends. Reported Hct target: Pediatric ~35%. Adult: no established hematocrit endpoint.
  • Stop criteria (immediate): Air detected / air entrainment risk. Inability to maintain positive arterial line pressure. Hemodynamic instability.
  • Anticoagulation: Maintain adequate anticoagulation throughout MUF (commonly with ACT monitoring).
  • Inflammation mediator removal (current perspective): MUF may reduce circulating cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-8), but evidence for meaningful improvement in hard clinical outcomes is limited. Practical emphasis remains fluid removal + hemoconcentration.
  • Total body water (pediatric): MUF is reported to reduce total body water after CPB; brain-specific outcomes were not reported in the reviewed source.
  • Temperature management: Blood can cool in the external MUF loop; for runs >~10 min, monitor core temperature and consider using a heat exchanger to prevent unintended hypothermia.
  • References: AmSECT Standards and Guidelines for Pediatric and Congenital Perfusion Practice (2019, Guideline 16.1 "should be utilized"; current update: "should be considered", unless contraindicated). 2024 EACTS/EACTAIC/EBCP Adult CPB Guidelines (section 7.4, ultrafiltration: Class I, Level A). See Sources & evidence levels below.

Sources & evidence levels

Evidence last reviewed: 2026-11-27. Each value shown in the cards is tagged by where it comes from. Tags describe the source type, not the strength of evidence or a treatment target.

  • Guideline Stated in a society guideline. Recommendation class and level are given when the guideline assigns one; otherwise the value comes from the guideline text.
  • Published Reported in a peer-reviewed study, review, or expert consensus.
  • Practice range No primary source was identified in the latest review. Treat as a practice convention and confirm against institutional protocol.
ACP — adult
  • Flow 8–12 mL/kg/min (≈0.6 mL/min/g brain) Published
    Both underperfusion and overperfusion impaired neurologic function in experimental studies [7]. Misfeld 2013 [5]. Other reported ranges: 10 mL/kg/min (Kazui) [9]; 6–10 mL/kg/min [6]; 10–15 mL/kg/min in the 2024 EACTS/EACTAIC/EBCP guideline text [1].
  • Perfusion pressure 40–60 mmHg (or 40–70) Published
    40–60 mmHg [5][6]; 40–70 mmHg right radial (Kazui) [9]. The 2024 EACTS/EACTAIC/EBCP guideline text cites a right radial pressure of 40–80 mmHg [1].
  • Perfusate temperature 23–28°C Published Guideline
    Brain perfused at 23–28°C [5]. Circulatory arrest under moderate hypothermia (20.1–28°C) combined with ACP should be considered for arch reconstruction (Class IIa, Level B) [1].
  • pH management: alpha-stat Guideline
    Alpha-stat favored over pH-stat in adults under high-moderate hypothermia (Class IIa, Level B) [1]; favored because it is reported to sustain cerebral autoregulation [5]. A source for "reduces embolization risk" was not identified, and that phrase was removed.
  • Duration up to ~80 min (observed) Published
    Observational registry of 2,008 elective arch operations with ACP: circulatory arrest up to at least 80 min was not associated with significant increases in mortality or permanent stroke [18]. This is an observed range in elective surgery, not an established universal safety limit. Pooled series: unilateral ACP about 30–50 min vs bilateral 86 to more than 164 min; bilateral perfusion is best documented once ACP is expected to exceed 40–50 min [8][5].
  • Monitoring: NIRS, EEG Guideline
    Bilateral NIRS is recommended in arch surgery or DHCA (Class I, Level C); transcranial Doppler may be considered (Class IIb, Level C) [1].
  • Hct 25–30% Published
    Review-based protocol [7]. The 2024 EACTS/EACTAIC/EBCP guideline text suggests Hb >7.5 g/dL and Hct >22% during arch surgery [1].
ACP — pediatric
  • Flow 40–80 mL/kg/min (reference 50–64) Published
    At least 40 mL/kg/min guided by neuromonitoring; published flows range 20–94 mL/kg/min; earlier series medians 63–64 mL/kg/min [10]. Neonatal series using 40–80 mL/kg/min, mean 56.6 ± 10.6 mL/kg/min [11].
  • Observed mean 46 ± 6 mL/kg/min (~25°C) Published
    Mean ACP flow 46 ± 6 mL/kg/min (target 40–60) in 61 children under 5 years, 72% aged ≤1 month, with ACP at about 25°C and pH-stat [19]. This is a mixed neonatal/pediatric cohort, not neonates only. A different protocol at 18°C with NIRS/TCD-guided flow reported 56.6 ± 10.6 mL/kg/min [11]; see also 57 ± 11 mL/kg/min [10].
  • <30 mL/kg/min risks hypoxic injury Published
    Flows below 30 mL/kg/min are inadequate for many patients, and below about 40 mL/kg/min may be inadequate; no single injury threshold is defined [10].
  • Perfusion pressure: titrate (often 20–25 mmHg) Published
    Reported radial pressures about 20–33 mmHg; right radial pressure ran about 10 mmHg higher than left [10]. Another series targeted an age-appropriate MAP of 25–55 mmHg [19].
  • Perfusate temperature 18–25°C Published
    ACP at 18°C [10][11] and at 25.0 ± 0.9°C (rectal) [19]. The ~25°C cohort reported preserved perioperative cerebral oxygenation; it notes that North American practice has typically used deep hypothermia with ACP or DHCA [19]. The previous "many programs favor ~25°C" wording had no identified source and was removed.
  • pH management: pH-stat Guideline
    pH-stat should be considered for neonates and infants undergoing hypothermia during CPB (AmSECT pediatric 2025 update, Guideline 9.1) [15]; used throughout bypass in [10].
  • Duration: no fixed limit Published
    No universal safe duration is established. The previous 20–48 min range had no identified source and was removed. Observed ACP times: median 48 min (range 10–123) at about 25°C [19]; mean 71 ± 28 min (range 5–121) at 18°C [11]; 76 ± 24 min [10].
  • Hct 30–35% Published
    Hct 30–35% during bypass and cooling [10].
RCP
  • SVC pressure ~20–25 mmHg (maintenance) Published Guideline
    Pressure-regulated RCP is favored over flow-regulated RCP to reduce the risk of brain edema [12]. In a canine study, 25 mmHg was the optimal venous pressure; cerebral blood flow did not rise further between 25 and 35 mmHg [13]. The 2024 EACTS/EACTAIC/EBCP guideline text cites an internal jugular vein pressure of 20 mmHg [1].
  • Flow 300–500 mL/min Guideline
    Guideline text: 100–500 mL/min, set to maintain the target venous pressure (no recommendation class) [1].
  • Monitoring: NIRS, EEG Guideline
    Bilateral NIRS is recommended in arch surgery or DHCA (Class I, Level C) [1].
HCA
  • Temperature categories Published Guideline
    Expert consensus (nasopharyngeal temperature): profound ≤14°C, deep 14.1–20°C, moderate 20.1–28°C, mild 28.1–34°C [3]. The 2024 EACTS/EACTAIC/EBCP guideline (Table 3, core rectal/bladder temperature) lists deep 14.1–20°C, low-moderate 20.1–24°C, high-moderate 24.1–28°C and mild 28.1–34°C [1]. The two use different measurement sites. The table uses the EACTS categories.
  • Model-estimated arrest time without cerebral perfusion Published
    Historical model estimates, not clinically validated safe limits, and not limits for arrest with ACP. A cerebral-metabolism model (Q10 2.3, assuming 5 min is safe at 37°C) estimates about 9 min at 30°C, 14 min at 25°C, 21 min at 20°C, 31 min at 15°C, and 45 min at 10°C without cerebral perfusion [4]. Values marked * in the table (28, 24 and 18°C) are calculated with the same assumptions: time = 5 × 2.3(37 − T)/10 min, rounded to whole minutes. Guideline text: isolated deep hypothermic arrest should not exceed 30–40 min [1].
  • Cooling time, CPB time and bleeding at warmer temperatures Published
    Not shown as a benefit in the table. In a small nonrandomized series of acute type A dissection (n = 74), 30°C with subclavian ACP (n = 18) was compared with 20–24°C using RCP (n = 18) or ACP (n = 38). CPB time was reported as significantly shorter (no values given), and 24-h chest-tube drainage was lower (703 ± 338 vs 1178 ± 820 and 1447 ± 802 mL) in the 30°C group [22]. Temperature and cerebral perfusion strategy differed together between groups, and transfusion was not reported. In the GOT ICE randomized trial (n = 282, mostly hemiarch, all with unilateral ACP; ≤20.0, 20.1–24.0 and 24.1–28.0°C), cooling time was shorter in the warmer groups (median 31 and 35 vs 42 min), but total CPB time was similar (158, 156 and 161 min). Total blood products were lower in the warmer groups (1442 and 1744 vs 2102 mL), without a higher rate of re-exploration for bleeding in the deep group [23]. The trial did not test temperatures above 28°C. Bleeding and transfusion outcomes are not the same as coagulopathy, and coagulation tests were not reported in the sources reviewed here.
  • Coagulopathy and use of deeper hypothermia Published
    Not shown as a temperature-specific risk in the table. A meta-analysis of nine mostly observational studies (n = 1,783) describes a potential association between deep hypothermic arrest and coagulopathy, notes that greater systemic morbidity with deep hypothermia has not been conclusively validated clinically, and found comparable reoperation for bleeding (DHCA 10.9% vs moderate HCA with ACP 13.3%; OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.43–1.69); blood loss and transfusion were not pooled [24]. In the GOT ICE trial (≤28°C), the deep group received more blood products without a higher re-exploration rate, and temperatures below 18°C were not analyzed separately [23]. Coagulation tests were not reported in these sources. The consensus statement describes profound hypothermia (≤14°C) as not frequently used; this does not apply to the 14.1–18°C deep range [3].
  • Cooling time at least 20–50 min Practice range
    No guideline minimum cooling time was identified. The guideline text limits the heat exchanger to arterial inflow gradient to 10°C [1].
  • Rewarming ≤0.5°C/min Guideline
    Reasonable when arterial outlet temperature is ≥30°C, with an arterial outlet to venous inflow gradient ≤4°C (both Class IIa, Level B) [2]. Cap arterial outlet temperature at 37°C (Class I, Level A) [1].
MUF
  • Pediatric: consider unless contraindicated Guideline
    The 2019 standards stated that MUF "should be utilized (unless contraindicated)" [14]. The current update changed Guideline 16.1 to "should be considered (unless contraindicated)" and gives no flow, duration, or hematocrit endpoint [15].
  • Adult: selective use Guideline
    MUF, or conventional ultrafiltration combined with MUF, is recommended in high-risk or anaemic patients to reduce chest-tube bleeding and transfusion (Class I, Level A) [1].
  • Pediatric flow 10–20 mL/kg/min Published
    Reported 10–15 mL/kg/min [16] and 10–30 mL/kg/min (veno-venous method) [17].
  • Pediatric duration 10–20 min; Hct ~35% Published
    MUF completed in 12.7 ± 1.7 min in one series [17]; target Hct 35% in a review [16]. The previous 40% upper value had no identified source and was removed.
  • Adult flow 150–300 mL/min, duration 10–15 min Published
    Study protocols, not guideline values: blood flow 150–300 mL/min through the filter for 10 min after CPB in adults with CKD undergoing CABG [20]; MUF for 15 min after CPB in elective CABG [21]. Blood flow through the filter and filtrate removal rate are different quantities.
  • Adult Hct: no established endpoint Practice range
    No source was identified for a general adult MUF hematocrit endpoint, so the previous ≥30–35% value was removed.
  • Circuit: A-V or V-V Published
    Arteriovenous MUF (arterial line to filter, return to the right atrium) is described in a pediatric review [16]; a veno-venous method is also described [17]. The adult studies cited used arterial and venous circuit lines [20][21]. No source was identified stating that one configuration is preferred.
  • Inflammatory mediators Published
    Reductions in IL-6, IL-8 and other mediators are reported, but results are mixed [16]; the guideline describes inflammatory-marker evidence in adults as inconsistent [1].
  • Total body water Published
    MUF is reported to reduce total body water after CPB [16]. A source for reduced cerebral water content was not identified, and that note was removed.
  • Safety: arterial line pressure, heat exchanger Published
    Continuous arterial line pressure monitoring and a heat exchanger in the MUF circuit are listed among MUF safety measures (the table previously said "required") [16].

Quick Reference FAQ

Can I use these quick-reference values as standalone treatment orders?

No. Quick-reference cards are educational and should be interpreted with institutional protocols, physician direction, and real-time patient monitoring.

What monitoring should be integrated when applying these reference ranges?

Integrate pressure trends, NIRS or EEG trends, and laboratory data while titrating flow or perfusion strategy.
